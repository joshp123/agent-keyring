# Threat model: what Agent Keyring protects against

Your agents need your passwords to sign in to websites and tools for you. Agent Keyring gives one agent one password for one task, for 30 minutes at most, when you approve it on your Mac with Touch ID, your iPhone with Face ID, or your Apple Watch. The aim is that a tricked agent or a rogue program on one of your computers cannot get a password you did not approve, and cannot make you approve something other than what you see.

## Where your passwords are

There is no vault of ours and no account. Every Mac and iPhone of yours is an equal: each keeps the signed list of devices and computers, the signed list of passwords and rules, and its own sealed copies. They keep each other up to date through your own iCloud private database (CloudKit container `iCloud.com.joshpalmer.AgentKeyring`, one record type, `VaultBlob`, in zone `AgentKeyringVault`). Nothing there is trusted for being there:

- **Passwords** are HPKE-sealed to the receiving device's Secure Enclave key, and signed by the sealing device's reply key, before they leave the device that exported them. Apple, we and your ISP see only ciphertext.
- **Members and the catalogue** (rules) are signed by a device's Secure Enclave signing key after Touch ID or Face ID. Every device checks each revision against the one before. Whoever can write to your iCloud cannot add a device, change a rule or downgrade a revision.
- **Each device's vault** (logins, imports and deletions, without secrets) is signed with its reply key and checked against Members and the writer named on the record. Other devices fold in its logins and deletions, never its rules: a rule comes only in a biometrically signed catalogue, so a forged vault cannot make a password Automatic.
- **Site and account names** are in CloudKit's encrypted fields: end-to-end encrypted with Advanced Data Protection; without it, Apple holds the keys.
- **Merging:** rules are last-write-wins per password (by time, then device key), so two devices end the same. A shared record (Members, catalogue) is written only if it is newer than the one in iCloud; otherwise the device takes that one and the change is made again.

Agent Keyring needs no Tailscale or other private network. Everything travels by three routes: your iCloud private database between your own Apple devices (above); Apple's push service, to wake a device; and a zero-data-retention relay of encrypted boxes, at `push.opentrawl.sh` or your own `agent-keyring-box`, between your devices and an agent computer. A box holds one AES-256-GCM message, keyed by the request, the one-time join code or the computer's mailbox seed, so the relay sees only random box IDs, access tokens (of which it keeps only hashes) and ciphertext. Box IDs and tokens travel in headers, never in URLs, and `push.opentrawl.sh` is run with no per-request logs. A join, answer or update box is deleted when it is picked up, and the relay stops serving any unread box when its time runs out (10 minutes for a join or an answer, 45 minutes for a grant, 7 days for an update mailbox); on the hosted AWS relay, DynamoDB then removes expired rows automatically, usually within a few days. The relay can drop or delay a message, but cannot read or forge a request, an answer, a join or a list.

Every approval has a grant at the relay, which Stop on any of your devices marks stopped. The computer reads the grant before every use of the approval (a read, a fill, a two-factor code) and refuses the use if the relay says it was stopped, cannot be reached, or holds no grant. A hostile relay could still answer with the grant as it was before the Stop, so a stopped approval could go on being used until its 30 minutes end; it cannot make an approval last longer than that.

A computer learns a rule change or a removed device from its update mailbox, which your Mac and iPhone each write after every change and once a day, and from each answer it receives (each answer carries the signed lists; an approval also carries that computer's own Automatic copies). It uses an Automatic copy on its own only while it holds a signed Freshness from one of your devices, at most 7 days old, so a relay that withholds updates can delay a change by at most a week. A computer keeps its own copy of a password only while the signed catalogue says Automatic, so switching one back to Ask removes it there with the next list.

A self-hosted relay must be reached over HTTPS with a certificate your iPhone and Apple Watch trust, such as one from Let's Encrypt: they refuse plain HTTP and self-signed certificates.

Every signature names its key by the key's own hash, and both the apps and the command check that the key in the list hashes to that name, so a forged list cannot pair one of your devices' IDs with someone else's key. A list of devices in iCloud counts only as far as your device can follow it, revision by revision, from one it holds or one your devices signed, so a forged revision is written over. A real list from another keyring (one set up while your device was offline) is never written over: whoever can write to your private database could leave one there and stop your devices updating theirs, though never forge one. Any other shared record with a higher revision counts only if one of your devices signed it. A Mac is woken for a computer's request by a silent push, opened with its own notice key; an outcome closes its panel only for the exact request it shows.

For waking an iPhone or Apple Watch over Apple Push Notification service (APNs), Apple requires the app developer's private `.p8` key. Public builds forward the already-encrypted push blob through a stateless blind push-signing Lambda at `https://push.opentrawl.sh` (AWS `eu-west-1`). It holds no user database and no decryption keys: it sees only opaque ciphertext, APNs device tokens, and timestamps (the same metadata Apple sees), and cannot read any site, username, or password, or forge an approval (the iPhone and Watch verify the requesting computer's signature against their signed `Members` list before showing a notification). Self-builders with their own Apple Developer `.p8` key can run `agent-keyring push-key` to push straight to Apple with no forwarder.

- **Your Mac:** holds every imported password that is not Blocked, sealed to this Mac's Secure Enclave key. Opening a password requires Touch ID (or macOS companion unlock). On a Mac without Touch ID whose owner opted in to Mac password mode, it requires the Mac's login password instead (or Touch ID, if a Touch ID keyboard is added later): weaker than Touch ID if a program can watch the password being typed.
- **Your iPhone (when paired, or set up on its own):** holds every non-Blocked password, sealed to its Secure Enclave key, opened only after Face ID. Set up with no Mac, it signs its own Members and catalogue and seals what it exports for every other device and computer.
- **Your iCloud private database:** holds only signed lists, signed vaults without secrets, and copies sealed to each device. A copy opens only on the device it is sealed to.
- **Your Apple Watch (when added):** holds its own copy of every password that is not Blocked, sealed to the Watch's Secure Enclave key and sent by its iPhone over WatchConnectivity. Copies the Mac seals for it pass through your iCloud private database as ciphertext only the Watch can open. None goes to a computer or the relay. A copy opens only while the Watch is unlocked on your wrist.
- **Enrolled computers (Automatic passwords):** a password set to Automatic is sealed to every enrolled computer's receiving key, with its two-factor seed. Anyone who controls that computer's user account can read Automatic passwords held there; keep sensitive accounts on Ask.

## What you see, and how far to trust it

| Shown | Where it comes from | Trust |
| --- | --- | --- |
| The password: site, account and name | Your own signed list of passwords, and the sealed copy, which carries the same name, sites and account inside it | Fact. A copy that does not match is refused before you see it. |
| The computer that asked | The request, signed by that computer's own key | Fact. |
| The agent, project and reason | Written by the agent | The agent's claim. A tricked agent can write anything here. |

## Threats

| Threat | Example | Defence | What remains |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| A trusted agent is tricked by a page or document | An agent reads a page that tells it to sign in to your bank | Your rules (Blocked or Ask); the request shows the real site; one approval covers one password for 30 minutes | You approve what you are shown. |
| A program on another enrolled computer asks | A script on a Linux server runs `agent-keyring lease` | It can ask only as that computer, and you see which computer asked. It cannot read Ask or Blocked passwords without your approval. | It can read Automatic passwords held on that computer, and ask like any agent there. |
| A rogue program on your Mac edits Agent Keyring's files | It renames your bank password to `example.com` or alters rules on disk | Your rules, signed device list and keys live in the app's own keychain, which other programs cannot edit. Each sealed copy carries its own name, sites and account, so if the list on disk has been tampered with, your touch opens nothing and no password is released. | It can ask like any agent on your Mac, and a changed list on disk can make macOS's dialog name the wrong site (your touch then releases nothing). |
| A stolen or replayed approval | Someone re-sends yesterday's approval | Each answer is signed by your approving device, covers one request's exact bytes, lasts 30 minutes, and seals the password to the asking computer's receiving key | None. |
| An agent asks again and again | 20 requests in a minute | Denying a password holds that computer off for 1 minute, then 5 minutes, then 15 minutes; one approval covers retries for 30 minutes | Fatigue is real. |
| A lost iPhone, Apple Watch or enrolled computer | Your iPhone or Linux server is stolen | iPhone and Watch keys require Face ID or wrist unlock. Remove any lost device or computer in Devices on your Mac to publish a new signed members list and delete its copies on your Mac. | Removing a lost device cannot erase copies already stored on it; change any Automatic passwords (and their two-factor seeds) that were on a compromised computer. |
| A program that knows your Mac login password, on a Mac in Mac password mode | An agent saw you type your password into a `sudo` prompt, or read it from a file | On a Mac with Touch ID none: its keys open only with Touch ID or the Watch. Mac password mode is an opt-in, offered only on a Mac without Touch ID, and the app states this cost before the keys are made. A Mac that only runs agents should have the command, never the app. | On a Mac in Mac password mode, such a program can answer macOS's dialog and approve its own requests. Its keys also accept Touch ID or a Watch if one is added later, but the password keeps working. |
| Someone at your unlocked Mac | | Out of scope: anyone at your unlocked Mac is you, as far as macOS is concerned | Lock your Mac. |
| Someone who can write to your iCloud | A stolen Apple Account session writes records into the vault zone | Every list is signed by one of your devices and checked against the one before; every copy is sealed and signed; vault records cannot carry rules; an iPhone restoring after a reinstall takes a list only if it signed one of its revisions itself, and joins a Mac only on a revision signed by the Mac whose iCloud Keychain invitation it proved | They can delete records (your devices still hold their own copies) and, without Advanced Data Protection, read which sites you have. |
| A forged push to the Mac | A computer sends a request notice to the Mac | The notice is sealed to the Mac's notice key and the request inside must be signed by a computer in Members, and name an answer box at a relay the Mac may use; it is checked exactly as the iPhone checks one | A computer in Members can ask, as it always could. |
| The relay misbehaves | The relay drops, delays or replays boxes, or answers with another box's contents | Every box is sealed with a key only the two ends hold, and everything inside is signed and checked against your own signed lists; a use whose grant cannot be read is refused; Automatic copies stop after 7 days without a fresh signed list | It can delay answers, list changes and Stop, so a request may wait or a use fail. |

## Limiting the damage

- **Browser extension vs `agent-keyring read`:** In Chrome, Chromium, Brave, Edge and Arc, the browser extension fills the form, submits and clears the fields without passing the password through the model, chat transcript or provider logs, and refuses to fill any site other than the one saved for that password. In Safari, built-in agent browsers, desktop apps and CLI tools where Chrome extensions cannot run, an approved agent signs in via `agent-keyring read`. The extension does not stop an agent you already approved from reading the password another way.
- **One-time codes:** The secret that generates two-factor codes stays on the approving device for Ask logins: the asking computer receives only the codes for the 30-minute approval window. (Automatic passwords include their two-factor seed on enrolled computers so unattended jobs can sign in.)
- **Automatic:** Each use is an ordinary 30-minute lease granted at once. It appears as active and Stop ends it. Set to Automatic only low-stakes passwords you are comfortable letting enrolled computers hold.
- **Blocked:** Setting a password to Blocked deletes every copy of it, on every Mac, iPhone, Apple Watch and computer. Agent Keyring keeps only its site, account and rule, so agents can see that it is blocked. If you unblock it, it has no password until you copy your passwords from Passwords again.
- **Your words reach the agent:** Deny with a short note ("use the gh command instead") and the agent receives your exact words.
